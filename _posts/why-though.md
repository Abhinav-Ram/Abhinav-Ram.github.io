We've collectively agreed to outsource emotional expression to commodities. Instead of the difficult, vulnerable work of showing love directly through time, attention, presence, words and actions, we've created a system where buying something "counts" as love. So we gaslight each other, “Of course I love you, I got you this expensive thing" and we accept it because rejecting it would mean confronting the uncomfortable truth that maybe we don't know how to show love directly anymore.

It's not just gift-giving, it's the entire infrastructure of how we signal value, belonging, and care in modern society. Every major life event has been financialized. If you really love them, spend $30k+, honour the deceased with an expensive casket and ceremony, celebrate achievement with elaborate parties, show success/appreciation with premium venues and champagne. 

Have we lost our inherent human value as a part of a social group? Or did we never have it in the first place? Maybe the question itself is wrong. Maybe we did have inherent value in smaller contexts, but scale killed it. In a village of 150 people, everyone knows your value through lived experience. In a city of millions, in a globalized economy, you need shorthand. You need legible signals. Money became that signal because it's universal, measurable, and doesn't require intimacy. So the horror isn't that we're shallow, it's that we've built a world too large and complex for genuine human connection to scale, and we've replaced it with financial transactions while pretending they're the same thing.

Because measurement and classification are how humans impose order on chaos. Without them, we'd drown in the infinite complexity of reality. But with them, we've created what we can call a discretized, oversimplified version of the world that's easier to navigate but fundamentally distorted. Creating in-groups and out-groups, reducing individuals to categories and stereotypes, turning quality into quantity, making baseless comparisons, creating hierarchies, ego clashes, competition and greed, reducing humans to metrics, calories, salaries, economy, material wealth, marks, IQ, EQ, lottery tickets.... LIST GOES ON. Some of these legitimately make decision-making tractable, but at the cost of nuance. They make progress legible, but define "progress" narrowly.

Why do we do this? Our brains can't process raw reality. We must simplify to function. Classification and measurement are dimensionality reduction. Without them, we'd be paralyzed. How do 8 billion people cooperate without knowing each other personally? Shared categories and metrics. Money, credentials, standardized tests, they're all attempts to create legible signals across vast distances. Once you can measure and classify, you can rank, compare, exclude, exploit. Those with power use metrics to justify hierarchy. "You scored lower, so you deserve less."

We started measuring things to understand them. But then we started optimizing for the measurements instead of the underlying reality. Why, though? Maybe because the alternative is unbearable. Living in full, unmeasured, unclassified reality means constant uncertainty, no comparisons, no shortcuts, and paralyzing complexity. The twist is, we’re doing both right now. We’re using classification (tribalism, pseudoscience, rat races) and measurement (the list) to critique classification and measurement. We’re discretizing the problem of discretization. Because that's all we can do. We can't escape the need to simplify. We can only be aware that we're doing it and choose our simplifications carefully.
